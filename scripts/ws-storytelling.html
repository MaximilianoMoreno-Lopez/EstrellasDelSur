Shared Stories Through Visual Storytelling

Bringing migrant and local young people together for the first time, using visual storytelling and humour to connect across languages.

Migrants + locals Young adults, 18+ Morocco & Senegal Triptychs + memes

This session was a little different from the previous workshops. While earlier activities had focused mainly on digital skills, employability and personal development within the migrant group, this workshop brought together the same ten young migrants from Morocco and Senegal with a group of local young people for the first time. The aim was not only to continue developing communication and digital skills, but also to create opportunities for interaction, mutual understanding and collaboration between young people with different backgrounds and life experiences.

To encourage genuine exchange, all activities were organised in mixed groups and pairs, combining local and migrant participants. Throughout the session, storytelling was used as a tool to help participants share aspects of their identity, experiences and perspectives in ways that did not rely heavily on language. The session was divided into two main parts, both centred around visual storytelling and creative expression using Canva.

Breaking the ice

As most participants had never met before, we began with a name-learning energizer. Everyone stood in a large circle while one facilitator directed the activity from the centre. The game required participants to quickly remember and call out each other's names, creating a lively atmosphere filled with laughter and friendly competition.

The activity worked exactly as intended. It broke the initial tension between the two groups and encouraged participants to interact from the very beginning. By the end of the game, people were already more relaxed, using each other's names naturally and feeling more comfortable working together.

Introducing ourselves through images

Before moving into collaborative storytelling, participants completed a simple Canva activity designed to help them introduce themselves visually. Each person was asked to create a small design using three images, icons or emojis that represented who they were, something they enjoyed, or something that was important to them.

This proved to be a very accessible starting point. Participants selected images connected to hobbies, sports, family, work, music, travel, religion and future aspirations. The activity generated natural conversations within the mixed groups, as participants explained the meaning behind their choices and discovered common interests with people they had only just met.

What was particularly interesting was that many participants who were initially hesitant to speak found it easier to communicate through images. The exercise reinforced one of the key ideas behind the workshop: that stories and identities can be expressed in many ways, not only through words.

Part one: telling stories together

The first major activity focused on visual storytelling through the creation of triptychs. Before designing their own stories, participants completed a communication challenge in pairs. One participant received a printed triptych while their partner had a blank Canva design. Without showing the original image, they had to describe what they could see while their partner attempted to recreate it.

The challenge quickly highlighted the importance of communication, active listening and cooperation. It also generated a lot of laughter as participants discovered how difficult it can be to describe visual information clearly.

Once familiar with the format, each mixed pair created their own triptych. The structure was simple: the first panel introduced the characters, represented by the participants themselves; the second presented a challenge or obstacle; and the third showed a solution or positive outcome.

The stories varied considerably. Some were inspired by real experiences, while others were more symbolic or imaginative. However, all required participants to discuss ideas together, agree on a narrative and find visual ways to communicate it. This collaborative process often led to conversations about personal experiences, aspirations and difficulties that might not otherwise have emerged.

When presenting their triptychs to the wider group, participants reflected on the meaning behind their work and explained the stories they had chosen to tell. Many discovered similarities in their experiences despite coming from very different cultural and personal backgrounds.

Part two: finding humour in challenges

The second half of the session took a lighter approach to storytelling through the creation of memes. Participants were invited to think about challenges they had faced in everyday life and transform them into humorous visual content.

The purpose was not to minimise difficulties but to create a comfortable environment in which people could talk about them. Humour provided an accessible way to discuss situations that might otherwise feel uncomfortable or sensitive, including language misunderstandings, cultural differences, job searching, bureaucracy and everyday frustrations.

Working in their mixed groups, participants designed memes using Canva and then shared them with the rest of the group. This activity generated some of the most animated discussions of the day. Participants frequently recognised themselves in each other's stories and responded with laughter, empathy and shared experiences.

What stood out was how effectively humour helped break down barriers. The activity encouraged participants to focus not only on what made them different, but also on the many challenges and experiences they had in common.

What we took away

Compared to the beginning of the programme, the migrant participants showed noticeably greater confidence when sharing ideas, working with digital tools and interacting with new people. The local participants also played an important role in creating an inclusive atmosphere, helping bridge language gaps and encouraging participation throughout the activities.

The use of images, visual storytelling and humour proved particularly effective in bringing people together. Rather than focusing on language limitations, participants were able to communicate through creativity, collaboration and shared experiences.

The outputs produced during the session, the personal identity boards created with three images, the collaborative triptychs and the memes developed by participants, have been collected and are available in the same project folder as this report.
